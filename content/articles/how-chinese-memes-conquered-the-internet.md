At some point in the last decade, without anyone signing a treaty, the world's internet users started speaking a little Chinese.

Not Mandarin — almost nobody learned Mandarin. What they learned was *gou liang* (dog food), *po fang* (breaking defense), *xian yu* (touching fish), and a family of acronyms — YYDS, XSWL, AWSL — that exist in no English dictionary and need no translation to be used. A slang dialect assembled in Shanghai dorm rooms and Guangzhou livestream studios now appears in the comment sections of Brooklyn, Berlin, and São Paulo. This is the story of how it traveled.

## The Laboratory

Every export starts with a surplus, and China's surplus was *speed*.

By the early 2010s, Chinese social media had hundreds of millions of users compressed into a handful of platforms — Weibo's public square, the bulletin boards, the livestream rooms — all competing to be funnier than each other in a language uniquely suited to compression. Chinese internet slang is built for the task: four characters can carry a story, an idiom, a moral, and an insult, and Mandarin's homophone-rich phonetics let numbers and letters stand in for entire phrases. When your language lets a single digit (6) mean "smooth," you can manufacture memes at industrial scale.

The laboratory also had a quality-control mechanism: the danmu. On video sites like Bilibili, comments fly across the screen in real time, and a phrase that survives ten thousand danmu without dying has been tested harder than any advertising slogan. What emerged from this Darwinian process was not random — it was *portable*. Chinese memes, it turned out, translated beautifully.

## The First Wave: Grammar Optional

The first slang to cross the border arrived around 2013–2015, and it crossed on the strength of pure wrongness. [No Zuo No Die](/no-zuo-no-die/) made no grammatical sense and conveyed something no English sentence could: the specific joy of watching someone provoke their own disaster. [You Can You Up, No Can No BB](/you-can-you-up/) compressed an entire argument about critics into six syllables of broken imperatives. [Smilence](/smilence/) — smile plus silence — was a portmanteau English itself should have invented centuries ago.

These phrases spread through Reddit, 9GAG, and Tumblr the way all great memes do: screenshots, T-shirts, bewildered journalists writing "have you seen this?" explainers. By 2014, *no zuo no die* had an Urban Dictionary entry. The remarkable thing was not that they were funny — the internet is funny — but that English speakers *kept using them after the joke faded*. Nobody says "all your base are belong to us" anymore. People still say no zuo no die.

## The Abbreviation Age

Then the export model changed. Around 2019, China began shipping acronyms.

[YYDS](/yyds/), [XSWL](/laugh-me-dead/), [AWSL](/ah-i-am-dead/) — four letters each, untranslatable, unpronounceable, and instantly useful. They arrived through fandom communities first: K-pop stans watching Chinese fan cams, gamers clipping Douyin moments, drama fans chasing subtitles. English speakers adopted them the way they had adopted "kawaii" — as emotional shorthand that felt more precise than anything domestic. When English gives you "LOL" and Chinese gives you XSWL ("laugh me dead"), the choice is not difficult. "LOL" died of overuse years ago; XSWL arrived pre-loaded with specificity.

This was a new phenomenon in linguistic history: a language exporting not *words* but *typing conventions*. The acronym YYDS is Mandarin, the alphabet is Latin, the context is global, and the speaker could be in Lagos or Lisbon. Scholars of pidgin languages would recognize the structure immediately — a stripped-down auxiliary dialect used for quick communication across cultures. The internet just built one in real time, on TikTok comment sections, for fun.

## The Supply Chain

None of this would have happened without logistics, and the logistics were platforms.

Douyin (TikTok's Chinese twin) generates the memes; TikTok distributes them; translation accounts bridge the gap; and the dictionary entries — the Urban Dictionary pages, the Know Your Meme explainers, and yes, sites like this one — give them permanent addresses. A phrase born in a Chengdu livestream at midnight can now be: clipped by morning, subtitled by afternoon, trending in London by evening, and printed on an Etsy mug by the weekend. The supply chain is so efficient that Chinese netizens sometimes find their own slang coming back to them from foreigners, polished and memed, like a linguistic boomerang.

This is the pattern to watch for the next decade. Chives get cut, fish get touched, defenses get broken, and every cycle, the vocabulary of the world's biggest internet population leaks a little further into everyone else's. The gatekeepers — dictionaries, teachers, style guides — keep arriving late to the border, stamping visas for words that crossed years ago.

## Why It Matters

Language historians used to track cultural power through books, newspapers, and radio. The new indicator is the comment section. If you want to know whose culture is being consumed, watch whose slang is being typed by people who cannot speak the language it came from.

By that measure, Chinese internet culture is doing something no non-English culture has done at this scale since Japanese anime: exporting a *dialect*. Not the language — the shorthand, the emotional abbreviations, the meme grammar. And unlike Japan's export, which arrived through official channels — studios, publishers, licensing deals — China's arrived through pure delinquency: too funny to ignore, too weird to forget, too useful to stop.

The dictionaries will catch up eventually. They always do. But the comment sections are already fluent.
