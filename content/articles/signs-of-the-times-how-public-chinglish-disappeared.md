For roughly thirty years, the streets of China produced the greatest accidental comedy in the history of public language.

A dry cleaner promising to "fuck the fragrantly." A menu offering "the wild husband and wife lung slice." A park sign gently advising visitors not to "disturb the tiny grass." A dish of [fried duck](/fuck-the-duck-until-exploded/) translated so catastrophically that it became the most famous four words in Chinglish history. These were not one-offs. They were an *era* — a thirty-year golden age of public Chinglish, when every street sign, hotel notice, and restaurant menu was a small lottery ticket in a national jackpot.

Then, quietly, the era ended. This is the story of why — and why the ending is more complicated than it looks.

## The Golden Age: 1980–2010

The boom began with the Reform and Opening. Suddenly, China had millions of signs that needed English — airports, hotels, restaurants, tourist attractions — and very few people who could write it. The task fell to whoever owned a dictionary and the most confidence: local teachers moonlighting for pocket money, employees' cousins who had "studied abroad," and above all the early electronic dictionaries, which translated character by character with the optimism of a slot machine.

The results were the product of a perfect storm. Chinese words carry multiple meanings; dictionaries picked the fun one. Sentences had no subject; signs inherited the confusion. Tone — the thing that separates "dry" from "fuck" in one famous dialect pronunciation — was invisible to machines and optional for humans. Nobody reviewed the output, because nobody *could* review the output. The sign went up, the foreigners photographed it, and an entire genre of coffee-table books was born.

This was Chinglish at its most democratic. The internet slang of the era was still a decade away. The pidgin phrases were a century gone. But the *signs* were everywhere, and they told a story the polished translations never could: a country sprinting toward the world faster than its language could follow, leaving hilarious footprints.

## The Cleanup: Two Olympics and a Government Decree

By the mid-2000s, the laughter had become an official embarrassment. Beijing was preparing for the 2008 Olympics, and nothing says "world-class city" like a sign forbidding visitors to "carefully slip and fall down." Municipal governments launched translation-correction campaigns. Beijing formed a "Standardization of Public English" task force. Shanghai followed for its 2010 Expo. Sign by sign, menu by menu, the most famous errors were hunted down and replaced with the dull, correct alternatives.

Linguists complained. Sinologists wrote op-eds mourning the coming sterility. But the critics misunderstood what was killing the signs. It was not the committees. It was the *machines* — and the machines had barely started.

## The Real Assassin: Your Phone

The committees fixed famous signs. Machine translation fixed *everything else*, everywhere, instantly, for free.

Consider what happened after 2016. Any restaurant owner could produce a flawless menu translation with a phone camera. Any sign painter could check a phrase before painting it. The error rate collapsed — not because humans got better at English, but because checking became cheaper than not checking. The golden age had required a specific condition: translation effort high, verification impossible. Smartphones deleted both. [Open water](/open-water/) became "boiled water." [Watch sister](/watch-sister/) became "cousin." The corrections were right, and something was lost that was also right.

This is the paradox every preservationist eventually faces: the artifact was a bug, the bug is fixed, and the fix is progress. Nobody should want the hospital signs back. And yet — the era's comedy was a genuine cultural document, a fossil record of how a language meets another language when the meeting is rushed, unedited, and sincere.

## The Archive Mission

Which is where the archive instinct comes in. The signs are disappearing from the streets faster than anyone is photographing them, and a dialect that exists only on street furniture is a dialect that dies with the furniture. Chinglish signage was never really about bad translation. It was about a specific historical moment — a country becoming visible to the world in real time, translation errors and all.

The gallery here exists for exactly that reason. Every photographed menu, every warning sign, every [dry-cleaned fragrance](/fuck-the-duck-until-exploded/) is a page from a diary that its author never knew he was keeping. The age of public Chinglish is ending. The least we can do is keep the receipts.

The last laugh, as always, belongs to the language itself. Machine translation polished the signs — and then the internet invented *no zuo no die*, which no machine would have dared to produce. Kill the errors, and the creativity moves underground. The grass is no longer tiny. The comedy is simply typed now.
