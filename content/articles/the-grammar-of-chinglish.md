Every Chinglish phrase you have ever laughed at was following a rule.

This is the secret the phrase "broken English" hides: Chinglish is not English with random damage. It is English operating under a different grammar — a consistent, learnable, surprisingly efficient grammar borrowed from Chinese. Once you can see the rules, the comedy changes. You stop seeing errors and start seeing a dialect, speaking with a Chinese accent at the level of *structure*. Here is the field guide.

## Rule One: The Articles Must Go

English is obsessed with *the* and *a*. Chinese has no articles at all — no "the," no "a," no "an" — and Chinglish honors this absence faithfully. *Long time no see* is not missing its articles by accident; it is executing a systematic policy. Where English says "I haven't seen you for a long time," Chinglish applies the Chinese frame directly: 好久不见 — very long time, not see. Two content words, zero grammar glue.

Drop the articles and sentences accelerate. "Give you some color to see see" gains its menacing rhythm precisely *because* nothing stands between the verb and its target. The missing "the" is not an absence. It is punctuation you can hear.

## Rule Two: Verbs Do Not Change Their Clothes

English verbs conjugate — walk, walked, walking — and mark time with their shape. Chinese verbs never change form; time is shown with little words like 了 (le), 过 (guo), and 在 (zai). Chinglish keeps this system and discards English's.

Hence *no can do* — the verb sits in its base form, the negation standing in front of it like a guard: 不行, *not can*. Hence *good good study, day day up*, where repetition does the work English assigns to adverbs. The result reads like verbs standing at attention: unbending, uncluttered, and — once you adjust — weirdly clear.

## Rule Three: One Item, One Word — Plurals Are Optional

Chinese nouns have no plural forms. One horse, two horse, many horse. Chinglish inherits this indifference, and it produces some of its most quotable lines. *People mountain people sea* is 人山人海 — mountain and sea of people — with the plurals simply declined to appear. The image is already plural by architecture; adding an "-s" would be redundant engineering.

This rule explains why Chinglish often sounds like a telegram from a more decisive world. English marks everything twice — once in the noun, once in the verb, once in the article. Chinglish marks it zero times and trusts the listener. Grammar as minimalism.

## Rule Four: Measure Everything

Here is the twist: where Chinglish removes English grammar, it *adds* Chinese grammar — and the addition is the tell that this is a system, not a mistake. Chinese requires measure words: not "a coffee" but "one *cup* coffee," not "a fish" but "one *strip* fish." When Chinglish speakers say "one *piece* advice" or "two *item* news," English speakers hear the error. What they are hearing is actually Chinese numerative grammar, faithfully installed.

The measure word is Chinglish's fingerprint. Articles disappear, plurals vanish, verbs freeze — and suddenly a *classifier* appears where English has never put one. No random damage could produce that pattern. Only another grammar could.

## Rule Five: Meaning Is Word Order, and Word Order Is Inflexible

Chinese is a topic-prominent language: state the subject, then say things *about* it, in an order that English finds presumptuous. Chinglish follows this road map. *You can you up, no can no BB* — condition first, command second, commentary last. The logic is arithmetic, not rhetorical. English asks "what happened?" and builds the sentence around the answer. Chinglish asks "what is the situation?" and lists it, in the order a mind would inventory it.

## Why the Rules Matter

Stand back and the field guide becomes something more than a comedy manual. Chinglish grammar is the grammar of *efficiency*: fewer markers, fewer agreements, more trust in word order and context. Linguists know this profile well — it is the profile of every language that evolved under pressure to be quick: trade pidgins, military slang, internet shorthand.

Which suggests the heretical conclusion: Chinglish was never bad English waiting to be corrected. It was a preview. The internet age has since pushed *actual* English in precisely this direction — dropping words, freezing verbs, trusting context, prioritizing speed. *No can do* did not fail to become standard English. It arrived early.
