## 📖 Origin Story
This Chinglish masterpiece is a brutally literal translation of Chinese New Year wishes for wealth and prosperity. In Chinese culture, phrases like "恭喜发财" (gōng xǐ fā cái) and "财源广进" (cái yuán guǎng jìn) are polite, poetic blessings for financial success. The Chinglish version strips away all poetry and replaces it with grammatical chaos:

* **"All money":** A direct, greedy grab for "所有的钱" (all the money) rather than the gentle concept of "prosperity."
* **"Back my home":** A catastrophic compression of "回到我家" (come back to my home). The missing preposition "to" transforms a blessing into a demand, as if the speaker is commanding cash to physically walk through their front door.

## 😂 Why It's Hilarious
The original Chinese phrases are elegant, indirect, and deeply rooted in cultural courtesy. You wish prosperity upon others, and the universe reciprocates. "All money back my home" throws this philosophy out the window and replaces it with bare-knuckle greed.

It sounds less like a New Year greeting and more like a bank robber's manifesto or a spell cast by a desperate wizard. The phrase "back my home" is particularly magical—it is not "come back," not "return to," but the imperative "back my home," as if money is a lost puppy that needs to be herded toward the speaker's house. The sheer shamelessness of demanding that *all* money—not some, not a little, but *all* of it—return to one's home is so aggressively selfish that it circles back into comedy.

## ✍️ Usage in Context
Modern netizens deploy this phrase during Chinese New Year, payday, or any situation where money is expected to change hands in their favor. It is the perfect ironic caption for receiving red envelopes, getting a tax refund, or watching a friend lose at poker.

**Example Conversation:**
* **A:** "I just got my year-end bonus!"
* **B:** "All money back my home! Transfer it here, I'll keep it safe."
* **A:** "That is not how bonuses work."
* **B:** "All money back my home is not a request. It is a law of nature."
