Every year, humans consume more *baijiu* than vodka, whiskey, rum, and gin combined. And yet, walk into a bar in London or New York and ask for a baijiu cocktail, and the bartender might look at you as if you've ordered a glass of moonshine. The world's most popular spirit has remained, for most of its history, stubbornly Chinese.

## 📖 Origin Story

*Baijiu* (白酒) literally means "white liquor" or "clear spirit"—a reference to its transparent appearance, not its color. The word entered English in the mid-20th century, primarily through diplomatic and trade contexts. Western journalists and businessmen in China needed a word for the fiery liquid that dominated every banquet, and they simply borrowed the Chinese name.

The spirit itself is ancient. Archaeological evidence suggests that distilled grain alcohol was being produced in China as early as the Han Dynasty (206 BCE–220 CE). By the Ming Dynasty, baijiu had become the national drink, with distinct regional styles emerging: the sauce-aroma (*jiangxiang*) of Moutai from Guizhou, the strong-aroma (*nongxiang*) of Luzhou Laojiao from Sichuan, and the light-aroma (*qingxiang*) of Fenjiu from Shanxi.

Moutai, the most prestigious brand, has played a starring role in China's diplomatic history. Zhou Enlai famously served it to Richard Nixon during the 1972 state visit that thawed Cold War relations. Nixon reportedly compared the taste to "liquid razor blades"—a backhanded compliment that only increased Moutai's mystique.

## 🌍 Why It Matters

*Baijiu* matters because it represents the final frontier of Chinese cultural exports. Tea arrived centuries ago. Kung fu arrived in the 1970s. But baijiu—despite being the most consumed spirit on Earth—has only recently begun to appear on Western back bars. The word's slow crossing reflects the drink itself: an acquired taste that demands patience, context, and often, a strong stomach.

The word also matters because it carries political weight. Moutai is often called "China's national liquor," and its price fluctuations are watched as closely as stock indexes. A bottle of vintage Moutai can cost thousands of dollars, making it a status symbol, an investment vehicle, and a gift of last resort for anyone trying to impress a Chinese official.
