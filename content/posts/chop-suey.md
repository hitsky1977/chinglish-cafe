No dish better captures the messy, creative, sometimes awkward birth of Chinese-American cuisine than *chop suey*. It was the most popular "Chinese" dish in the United States for nearly a century. It appears in classic Hollywood films, Tin Pan Alley songs, and countless family memories. And yet, ask a chef in Beijing or Shanghai what *chop suey* is, and you will likely receive a blank stare.

## 📖 Origin Story

The origin of chop suey is one of the most contested stories in American food history. The most famous legend claims it was invented in 1896 in New York City, when a group of Chinese diplomats visited the city and their chef, Li Hongzhang's private cook, had to improvise a meal from leftover ingredients. He stir-fried miscellaneous vegetables and meat together, and the dish was born. The name supposedly comes from the Cantonese *tsap seui* (杂碎), meaning "miscellaneous broken pieces."

Another theory argues that chop suey was actually invented by Chinese immigrant cooks in San Francisco's Chinatown, who created it as a way to use up kitchen scraps and appeal to American tastes. A third theory suggests it was based on a real dish from Taishan, the Guangdong region that sent most of the early Chinese immigrants to America.

Whatever its origin, chop suey exploded in popularity in the early 20th century. By the 1920s, chop suey houses dotted every major American city. The dish was cheap, filling, and mildly exotic—Chinese enough to feel adventurous, but tame enough for cautious palates. It kept Chinese restaurants in business through decades of anti-Chinese prejudice and immigration exclusion.

## 🌍 Why It Matters

*Chop suey* matters because it forces us to ask: what makes food "authentic"? Food purists dismiss chop suey as "not real Chinese food," but that judgment ignores history. It was cooked by Chinese people, in Chinese restaurants, using Chinese techniques, for a century. If that does not make it Chinese, what does?

The word also matters as a cultural survival strategy. When Chinese immigrants were barred from most professions by racist laws, restaurants were one of the few businesses they could open. Chop suey was their compromise: a dish that kept their restaurants alive while making white customers feel comfortable. It was not inauthentic—it was adaptive.
