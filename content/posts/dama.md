In the West, "auntie" is a family title. In China, *dama* is a social phenomenon. It refers to a specific demographic—middle-aged and elderly Chinese women—who possess an unstoppable combination of free time, tight-knit social networks, and terrifying purchasing power. When *The Wall Street Journal* coined the term "Dama" in 2013, it was not being polite. It was being accurate.

## 📖 Origin Story

*Dama* (大妈) literally means "big mother" or "elder aunt," a term of address for women roughly between 45 and 70. But the word carries layers of cultural baggage: the *dama* is the woman who haggles at the wet market, who pushes to the front of the bus line, who organizes neighborhood gossip networks, and who dominates public squares with synchronized dancing and portable speakers.

The word exploded into global English in 2013, when Chinese *dama* shocked international markets by collectively buying hundreds of tons of gold during a price dip. *The Wall Street Journal* ran a headline about the "Chinese Dama" gold rush, and the term stuck. Suddenly, Western finance reporters needed a word for "middle-aged Chinese women who move markets," and "auntie" was not going to cut it.

## 🌍 Why It Matters

*Dama* is one of the few Chinese words adopted by English-language media to describe a specific demographic with specific economic and social power. It is not a slur, nor is it entirely complimentary—it is a recognition that this group behaves in ways that defy Western stereotypes of aging women.

The *dama* phenomenon also challenges how the West views Chinese consumer power. These are not wealthy elites or tech-savvy youth. They are retirees and homemakers who, through sheer collective will, can crash gold prices, empty supermarket shelves, and occupy every public park in China. English needed a word for that. It took *dama*.
