In the Cantonese-speaking world, *dim sum* literally means "to touch the heart"—a poetic way of saying "a little something to delight the spirit." In the English-speaking world, it means Sunday brunch with steamer baskets, tea, and the gentle anxiety of pointing at mysterious dumplings as they roll past on a trolley.

## 📖 Origin Story

The tradition of *dim sum* originated in the teahouses of Guangzhou and Hong Kong, where travelers and merchants would pause for tea and small snacks. The culture of *yum cha* (drinking tea) became inseparable from the food that accompanied it: har gow, siu mai, char siu bao, and dozens of other bite-sized creations.

As Cantonese immigrants established Chinatowns across North America in the mid-20th century, *dim sum* followed. The trolley service—waitresses pushing carts of steaming bamboo baskets—became a defining feature of the Western *dim sum* experience. It was theatrical, communal, and slightly chaotic: exactly the kind of dining experience that fascinated American diners accustomed to individual plated meals.

## 🌍 Why It Matters

*Dim sum* was one of the earliest Cantonese food terms to enter mainstream English, alongside *chow mein* and *wonton*. But it exported more than a menu—it exported a dining philosophy. The idea of sharing many small dishes, eating slowly, and letting the table become a social event rather than a fuel stop was, for many Westerners, a revelation.

Today, *dim sum* has escaped Chinatown. It appears in food halls, fusion restaurants, and Michelin guides. The word itself has become shorthand for any small-plate, shared dining experience, even when the food is not remotely Cantonese.
