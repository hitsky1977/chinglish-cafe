## 📖 Origin Story
"龙生龙，凤生凤，老鼠的儿子会打洞" (lóng shēng lóng, fèng shēng fèng, lǎo shǔ de ér zi huì dǎ dòng) is a brutally honest Chinese proverb about heredity, social class, and family destiny. It observes that dragons beget dragons, phoenixes beget phoenixes, and the son of a mouse knows how to dig holes. It is the Chinese equivalent of "like father, like son," but with significantly more zoological variety and considerably less optimism for social mobility.

The Chinglish translation performs a multi-species grammatical massacre:

* **"Dragon born dragon":** A literal, passive-voice disaster. In Chinese, "龙生龙" means "dragons give birth to dragons." The Chinglish version sounds like a dragon is being born from a dragon, or perhaps a dragon is giving birth to itself.
* **"Chicken born chicken":** The fatal downgrade. The original word is "凤" (fèng), the mythical phoenix—a creature of majesty equal to the dragon. The translator, apparently unfamiliar with mythical birds, substituted "chicken." The phoenix has been demoted to poultry.
* **"Mouse' son can make hole":** A punctuation catastrophe. The original says "老鼠的儿子" (the mouse's son). The Chinglish version uses "mouse'"—an apostrophe floating in the wrong place, creating a possessive that looks like a typo and sounds like a hiccup. "Make hole" transforms a natural instinct into a construction project.

## 😂 Why It’s Hilarious
The original proverb is a sharp, somewhat cynical observation about how family background shapes destiny. It is the kind of thing a grandmother says when she sees a child misbehaving exactly like their father. The Chinglish version sounds like a livestock breeding manual written by someone who failed biology, English, and punctuation simultaneously.

"Dragon born dragon, chicken born chicken, mouse' son can make hole" reads like a genetic engineering report from a very confused laboratory. Instead of a profound statement about human nature and inherited traits, it sounds like a farmer checking his inventory: dragons produce dragons (good), chickens produce chickens (acceptable), and mice produce... construction workers?

The "chicken" substitution is the comedic crown jewel. The phoenix, a divine bird of grace and rebirth, has been replaced by a barnyard animal that cannot even fly properly. It is the linguistic equivalent of replacing Romeo with a neighborhood pigeon. The dragon gets to remain a dragon, but the phoenix becomes dinner.

"Mouse' son" is the grammatical cherry on top. The apostrophe is so lost that it appears to be trying to escape the sentence entirely. It turns a simple possessive into a visual puzzle: is the mouse named Mouse? Is the son named Mouse? Is the hole being made by a son who belongs to a mouse, or by a mouse who belongs to a son?

## ✍️ Usage in Context
Modern netizens use this phrase to roast friends who display unmistakable family traits—temper, habits, career choices, or the inability to be on time. It is the perfect way to say "you are exactly like your parents" while sounding like you are reading from a wildlife documentary script.

**Example Conversation:**
* **A:** "My dad was a lawyer, my grandfather was a lawyer, and I just passed the bar exam."
* **B:** "Dragon born dragon, chicken born chicken, mouse' son can make hole."
* **A:** "Did you just call me a mouse?"
* **B:** "I called you a hole-maker. Congratulations on the bar exam, counselor."
