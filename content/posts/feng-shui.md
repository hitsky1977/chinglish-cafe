Most people associate *feng shui* with moving furniture around or hanging crystals. In reality, it is a complex system of Chinese cosmology that governs how energy (*qi*) flows through the natural and built environment. The West did not borrow a decorating trend—it borrowed a worldview, then turned it into a verb.

## 📖 Origin Story

*Feng shui* (风水) literally translates to "wind and water"—the two primary forces that carry *qi* across the landscape. Its principles date back over 3,000 years to early Chinese burial and settlement practices, where geomancers would analyze mountain ranges, river curves, and celestial alignments to find auspicious locations.

Western missionaries and traders first encountered *feng shui* in the mid-19th century, often dismissing it as superstition. The concept remained obscure until the 1970s, when a wave of Chinese immigration to North America and the concurrent New Age movement created the perfect conditions for *feng shui* to cross over. Books, consultants, and home-improvement shows transformed an ancient metaphysical science into a mainstream design language.

## 🌍 Why It Matters

*Feng shui* is one of the earliest Chinese metaphysical concepts to achieve genuine mainstream acceptance in the West. It entered the real estate industry ("This house has good feng shui"), architecture, and interior design. English speakers even use it as a verb: *"We need to feng shui this room."*

What makes its journey remarkable is that the West did not merely adopt the word—it adopted the underlying logic. The idea that your physical environment directly affects your fortune, health, and mental state is now so commonplace in wellness culture that many forget it originated in Han-dynasty burial manuals.

## ✍️ Usage in Context

**The Design Context:**
