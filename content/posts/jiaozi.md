For decades, English speakers called them "dumplings"—a catch-all term that lumped *jiaozi* together with Polish pierogi, Japanese gyoza, and Italian ravioli. But *jiaozi* is not just any dumpling. It is a specific shape, a specific folding technique, and a specific ritual tied to Chinese New Year. In the 2010s, as food culture globalized and authenticity became currency, English finally started calling it by its real name.

## 📖 Origin Story

*Jiaozi* (饺子) dates back over 1,800 years to the Eastern Han dynasty, traditionally credited to the medical sage Zhang Zhongjing, who supposedly invented them as ear-shaped warming packets to treat frostbitten villagers. The crescent shape resembles ancient Chinese gold ingots, making *jiaozi* a symbol of wealth and prosperity—hence their non-negotiable presence at Lunar New Year tables.

The word entered global English gradually. Chinese restaurants abroad initially listed "dumplings" or "potstickers" on English menus. But as food bloggers, cooking shows, and YouTube tutorials democratized culinary knowledge, the word *jiaozi* began appearing in English food writing. By the 2020s, major supermarkets in the West were selling frozen "Jiaozi" alongside "Gyoza" and "Dumplings"—a quiet admission that the generic term was no longer enough.

## 🌍 Why It Matters

*Jiaozi* represents a shift in how English borrows food words. Earlier generations of Chinese food in the West hid behind translated names: "egg rolls," "fortune cookies," "Peking duck." *Jiaozi* arrived at a moment when English-speaking food culture was confident enough to learn foreign words rather than simplify them.

The word also carries ritual weight that "dumpling" cannot convey. You do not simply eat *jiaozi*—you fold them with family, you hide a coin inside one for luck, and you eat them at midnight to welcome the new year. By keeping the word *jiaozi*, English preserved some of that cultural specificity.
