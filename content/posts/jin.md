Walk into any Chinese wet market, and you will hear a word that has been used for over two thousand years: *jin*. It is the unit of weight that grandmothers use to buy pork, that fishmongers shout across stalls, that farmers still use to describe their harvest. And while it has been officially "replaced" by the metric system, the *jin* refuses to die.

## 📖 Origin Story

*Jin* (斤) is one of the oldest units of weight in the world, with evidence of its use dating back to the Shang Dynasty (1600–1046 BCE). Originally, its weight varied wildly by region—a jin in one province might be completely different from a jin in another. This chaos continued until the Qin Dynasty's first emperor, Qin Shi Huang, attempted to standardize weights and measures across his empire.

The modern *jin* was standardized in 1959 by the People's Republic of China at exactly 500 grams, or half a kilogram. This made conversions easy—two jin equal one kilogram—but it also stripped the word of its historical variability. A *jin* in Tang Dynasty Chang'an was closer to 680 grams. A *jin* in Qing Dynasty Guangzhou might have been different still.

The word entered English through trade and travel writing, but it remains far less common in English than *li* or *yuan*. English speakers who know the word *jin* are likely to be China specialists, frequent travelers, or readers of Chinese literature. It has not crossed into mainstream English usage—yet.

## 🌍 Why It Matters

*Jin* matters because it reveals how language and the body are connected. In China, people do not think in abstract kilograms when buying food; they think in *jin*, a unit tied to human scale. A *jin* of meat is roughly what one person might eat in a few days. A *jin* of fruit is a bag you can carry home. The *jin* is intuitive in a way that grams and kilograms are not.

It also matters as a symbol of cultural persistence. The Chinese government has spent decades promoting the metric system, yet the *jin* endures in markets, kitchens, and conversations. It is a quiet rebellion against standardization—a reminder that language, like weight, is ultimately about what feels right in the hand.
