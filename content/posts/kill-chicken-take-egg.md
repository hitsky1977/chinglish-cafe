## 📖 Origin Story

Once upon a time, in a village that existed in roughly every culture on Earth, a farmer's chicken began laying eggs. The farmer was delighted. He sold the eggs, bought grain with the money, and congratulated himself on his new income stream. Then greed arrived for its meeting.

The eggs, the farmer reasoned, must come from somewhere *inside* the chicken. If one egg a day was good, surely everything the chicken would ever produce was already in there, waiting. So he killed the chicken, opened it up, and found — nothing. No reserve supply. No egg warehouse. Just a chicken, which is the only thing in the world that makes eggs, and which he no longer had.

The tale survives in Aesop too, except the Greek version stars a goose and golden eggs — aristocratic poultry, mythological wages. The Chinese version chose the chicken: practical, peasant, and significantly less magical. The moral is identical. The poultry is just more honest.

## 🤓 Analysis & Usage

The existence of an almost identical Aesop fable makes this entry a cross-cultural diplomatic moment. Two civilizations, separated by deserts and oceans, independently decided that the dumbest economic plan imaginable involved poultry. English got the goose; Chinese got the chicken. "Kill chicken take egg" is therefore not just Chinglish — it is evidence that greed is the most internationally consistent force in human history.

The Chinglish version also has a certain boardroom directness. "Killing the golden goose" sounds like a tragedy in a palace. "Kill chicken take egg" sounds like a strategy that got approved at a quarterly meeting. It is the idiom you reach for when a company monetizes its loyal users into leaving, when a boss cuts the training budget that produced his best staff, or when anyone trades tomorrow for today and calls it growth.
