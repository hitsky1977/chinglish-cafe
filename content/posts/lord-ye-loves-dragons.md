## 📖 Origin Story

The story comes from the Han Dynasty, from a nobleman named Lord Ye who was obsessed with dragons. His obsession was not casual. It was total. He carved dragons into his furniture, painted dragons on his walls, embroidered dragons onto his robes, and commissioned dragon-shaped jewelry. His servants wore dragon motifs. His horses had dragon bridles. His house was a temple to dragons. He talked about dragons constantly. Dragons, he told everyone, were the most magnificent creatures in existence. He loved them more than anything.

The actual dragons heard about this. Real dragons — the kind that live in clouds and rivers, the kind that control rain and thunder — decided to visit him. One day, a dragon descended from the sky and landed in Lord Ye's courtyard. Its scales glittered. Its breath steamed. Its eyes were ancient and intelligent. It had come to meet its biggest fan.

Lord Ye screamed and ran. He hid under his bed. He wet himself. He begged the dragon to leave. The dragon, confused and deeply hurt, flew away. Lord Ye never spoke of dragons again. His dragon carvings were painted over. His dragon robes were burned. And the phrase "Lord Ye loves dragons" entered the language as a permanent description of people who love the idea of something but are terrified by the reality.

## 🤓 Analysis & Usage

English speakers call this "all talk, no action" or "fake it till you make it," but neither captures the specific emotional arc of the original story. Lord Ye did not merely fail to follow through. He actively promoted himself as dragon-obsessed. He built an identity around dragons. The dragon came to him specifically because of this identity — and the identity collapsed instantly under contact with the real thing.

"Lord Ye loves dragons" describes not hypocrisy but a specific type of performative enthusiasm. It applies to people who claim to love camping but panic without Wi-Fi, who call themselves entrepreneurs but have never made a sale, or who post daily about mental health awareness but mock their friends for going to therapy. The dragon is not testing their courage. The dragon is testing whether their public identity has any relationship to their private capacity.

The Chinglish version also introduces a title of unexpected dignity. "Lord Ye" sounds like a character from Arthurian legend or a minor noble in a Jane Austen novel. The idea that this dignified figure wet himself at the sight of his own obsession creates a comic catastrophe of almost Shakespearean proportions. He is not a villain. He is a fool — and fools are always funnier than villains.

## ✍️ Usage in Context

Modern Chinese use 叶公好龙 to describe anyone whose enthusiasm for a concept evaporates on contact with its reality. It applies to startup founders who love "disruption" until their own industry is disrupted, or to activists who love "revolution" until it requires them to change their own behavior.
