In 742 CE, the Tang Dynasty emperor Xuanzong wanted to impress his favorite concubine, Yang Guifei. She loved lychees — the small, translucent-fleshed fruits that grew only in the subtropical south, a thousand miles from the capital. The emperor's solution was extreme: he established a dedicated imperial courier service using the fastest horses in the empire, galloping day and night along mountain roads, changing mounts at relay stations, all so that Yang Guifei could eat lychees while they were still fresh. A single fruit could cost more than a peasant's annual income. The concubine smiled. The empire wobbled. And a fruit became a legend.

## 📖 Origin Story

The lychee (*Litchi chinensis*) is native to southern China and has been cultivated for at least two thousand years. Chinese poets wrote about it before the Common Era. Imperial courts prized it as a luxury item. And when Portuguese explorers and traders arrived in southern China in the sixteenth century, they encountered a fruit they had never seen before — small, rough-skinned, with flesh like perfumed jelly.

The Portuguese recorded the Cantonese name as *leechias* or *lichias*, which entered English in the late sixteenth or early seventeenth century as "lichee," "litchi," and eventually "lychee." The modern spelling "lychee" reflects a compromise between Cantonese pronunciation and English orthographic habits — a classic example of how Chinese words are slowly domesticated by English phonology.

By the nineteenth century, lychees were being cultivated in British India, French Indochina, and eventually Hawaii and Florida. But the best lychees — the ones that inspired the emperor's courier service and the concubine's smile — still come from Guangdong and Fujian, where they have grown since before Rome fell.

## 🌍 Why It Matters

Lychee matters because it is one of China's oldest agricultural exports — a fruit that traveled the world not through conquest but through desirability. Unlike tea or silk, which were industrial products that required cultivation knowledge, lychees simply made people want more of them. The Portuguese wanted them. The British wanted them. The French wanted them. And the Chinese, who had been eating them for two millennia, were happy to sell.

The word also matters because it carries a specific sensory memory. English speakers who know lychees do not think of them as generic "tropical fruit." They think of a precise combination of sweetness, floral fragrance, and faint acidity — a flavor profile so distinctive that "lychee" has become a standard note in perfumery and cocktail mixing. The word has escaped the produce aisle and entered the vocabulary of luxury.
