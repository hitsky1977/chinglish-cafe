In 1920s America, no self-respecting suburban hostess could be caught without a *mahjong* set. Department stores sold them in leather cases. Department store windows displayed them alongside fur coats and gramophones. For a brief, glittering moment, *mahjong* was not a Chinese game—it was the American pastime.

## 📖 Origin Story

*Mahjong* (麻将) emerged during the mid-to-late Qing dynasty, though its exact origins are debated—some trace it to card games of the Ming dynasty, others to court games played by sailors along the Yangtze River. The name itself is thought to derive from the Mandarin word for "sparrow" (*maque*), allegedly because the clacking of tiles sounds like chattering birds.

The game's journey to English began in the international settlements of Shanghai, where American expatriates encountered it in the early 1920s. Joseph Park Babcock, an American oil executive stationed in China, published *Rules of Mah-Jongg* in 1920, standardizing the game for Western audiences. By 1923, *mahjong* was a craze: Abercrombie & Fitch sold sets, the *New York Times* published strategy guides, and Hollywood stars were photographed hunched over tile racks.

## 🌍 Why It Matters

*Mahjong* is arguably the most successful Chinese game export in history. Unlike *Go* (weiqi), which remained niche and intellectual, *mahjong* became genuinely mainstream in the West before retreating into ethnic community halls and retirement homes. Its 1920s popularity proved that Chinese cultural products could capture the American mainstream—not through assimilation, but through sheer addictive gameplay.

The word has also become a cultural marker. In American Jewish communities, *mahjong* became a weekly ritual so entrenched that many assume the game itself is Jewish. In Chinese diaspora communities, it remains the soundtrack of family reunions—the clack of tiles drowning out arguments and binding generations together. English borrowed the word, but different cultures made it their own.
