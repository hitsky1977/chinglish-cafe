In the 1920s, Shanghai's most fashionable women did not wear Western gowns. They wore *qipao*—form-fitting silk dresses with high collars and slits that revealed just enough to scandalize and captivate. A century later, the word has entered English not as a costume, but as a category of elegance.

## 📖 Origin Story

*Qipao* (旗袍) literally means "banner robe," originating from the Manchu *banner people* who ruled China during the Qing dynasty (1644–1912). Originally a loose, straight-cut robe worn by Manchu women, it was transformed in 1920s Shanghai into the figure-hugging silhouette the world recognizes today. Tailors narrowed the waist, raised the collar, and added slits—creating a garment that was simultaneously traditional and daringly modern.

The word entered English through two channels: Cantonese-speaking immigrants called it *cheongsam* (长衫), while Mandarin speakers brought *qipao*. Fashion magazines in the 1960s, captivated by the garment's cinematic elegance, began using both terms. Today, *qipao* has largely won out in global fashion discourse, though *cheongsam* still lingers in Cantonese communities and older English texts.

## 🌍 Why It Matters

*Qipao* is one of the few Chinese fashion terms to achieve genuine international recognition. Unlike "kimono" or "sari," which entered English as exotic curiosities, *qipao* arrived as high fashion. It was worn by Maggie Cheung in *In the Mood for Love*, by celebrities on red carpets, and by brides seeking something more distinctive than a white gown.

The word also carries a complex cultural identity. For some, it represents colonial fusion—Manchu clothing, remade by Han Chinese tailors, popularized by Western fashion. For others, it is the definitive symbol of Chinese feminine grace. By adopting *qipao* into English, the world accepted a garment that refuses to fit neatly into any single cultural box.
