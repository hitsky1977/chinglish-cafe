In English, a "running dog" once meant a hunting hound. In Maoist China, it meant something far more vicious: a craven servant of imperialism, a traitor who served foreign masters against their own people. For a brief, fiery period during the Cold War, the Chinese definition challenged the English one—and won a place in the dictionary.

## 📖 Origin Story

*Zou gou* (走狗) has existed in Chinese for millennia, describing a kept thug or hired henchman—literally, a "running dog" who does his master's bidding. The insult gained explosive political charge in the 1950s and 1960s, when Chinese state media used it relentlessly in English-language propaganda to denounce Western powers and their allies.

Publications like *Peking Review* and English-language broadcasts from Beijing repeatedly deployed "running dog of imperialism" and "running dog of capitalism." Western journalists and scholars, reading these translations, found the phrase strikingly vivid. Unlike dry political jargon, "running dog" carried an almost cartoonish contempt—a snarling, servile creature scurrying after its master.

## 🌍 Why It Matters

*Running dog* is a rare case where English borrowed not a Chinese cultural concept, but a Chinese **political rhetoric**. It did not enter English through cuisine, philosophy, or martial arts—it entered through ideology.

While the phrase has faded from active political speech, it remains a fascinating specimen in the museum of Cold War language. It demonstrates how translation can be weaponized, and how a literal rendering of an insult can sometimes be more effective than a "smoother" equivalent. "Lackey" sounds quaint; "running dog" sounds like it wants to bite you.

## ✍️ Usage in Context
