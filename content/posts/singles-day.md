Every November 11, humanity conducts the largest experiment in consumer desire ever attempted, and it began as a joke about being single. Singles' Day — 11.11, four lonely ones standing in a row — started in 1993 as a sort of anti-Valentine's Day for Chinese university students, a day for the uncoupled to buy each other dinner and commiserate. Two decades later, it generates more sales in one day than Black Friday and Cyber Monday combined. No holiday in history has been monetized this fast, this completely, or with this much irony.

## 📖 Origin Story

The legend places the founding at Nanjing University, where four single male students (in the most repeated version) decided November 11 — written 11.11, resembling "bare branches" (光棍), a northern expression for bachelors — deserved its own observance. The holiday spread through Chinese campuses in the 1990s as gentle self-mockery: single people treating themselves, joking about their status, buying their own gifts.

Then, in 2009, Alibaba noticed. The e-commerce giant rebranded the day as a shopping festival for singles to "treat yourself," launched it with 27 participating merchants, and watched it explode. By the mid-2010s, Singles' Day (双十一, "Double Eleven") had become a national consumption ritual with a gala concert, a countdown show, and single-day sales figures that global economists now treat as a health indicator for the Chinese consumer. The English name "Singles' Day" traveled with the business press coverage, entering international vocabulary as executives worldwide tried to understand why a country could sell $30 billion of goods in 24 hours while their own retail holidays strained for a tenth of that.

## 🌍 Why It Matters

Singles' Day matters to English as the only major global festival with a *marketing-department birth certificate*. Christmas, Ramadan, and Diwali grew organically over centuries; Singles' Day was engineered in eleven years, from campus joke to economic super-event. It gave English a new concept: the commercially manufactured holiday so successful it became culturally real.

It also matters as a window into modern China. The holiday's success was built on the very demographic it mocked — young, urban, single, digitally native consumers with disposable income and no family to spend it on. The joke became the economy. Somewhere in that transition, "bare branches" stopped meaning lonely and started meaning *market segment*.
