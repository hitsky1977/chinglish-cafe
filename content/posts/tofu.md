*Doufu*. That is what it is called in Mandarin. But when English speakers order it, they say *tofu*—a pronunciation that reveals exactly how this ancient food arrived in the West. It did not come from Beijing. It came from Guangdong, carried by Cantonese immigrants who brought their woks, their recipes, and their word for bean curd.

## 📖 Origin Story

*Tofu* is the Cantonese pronunciation of 豆腐 (dòu fu), which literally means "bean curd." The food itself was invented in China over 2,000 years ago, traditionally credited to Prince Liu An of the Han dynasty. It spread throughout East Asia, becoming *dubu* in Korea, *tahu* in Indonesia, and *tofu* in Japan.

English encountered *tofu* through Japanese cuisine in the late 19th century, but the word's true popularization came with the wave of Cantonese immigration to North America and the subsequent boom in Chinese restaurants. By the 1970s and 1980s, *tofu* had become a staple of health food stores and vegetarian cookbooks. Today, it sits in every mainstream supermarket in the English-speaking world.

## 🌍 Why It Matters

*Tofu* is a case study in how English borrows food words from the people who cook the food, not from the official language of the country. English did not adopt the Mandarin *doufu*. It adopted the Cantonese *tofu* because the cooks and restaurant owners who introduced it to America spoke Cantonese.

The word also represents one of China's most successful culinary exports. Unlike *dim sum* or *chow mein*, which require a restaurant, *tofu* is an ingredient. It has been incorporated into Western cooking so thoroughly that many English speakers do not even associate it with China anymore. It is simply *tofu*—a global food with a Chinese name.
