Every language has a word for people with more money than taste. English has "nouveau riche." French has *parvenu*. Chinese has *tuhao*—and thanks to the internet, the rest of the world now has *tuhao* too.

## 📖 Origin Story

*Tuhao* (土豪) is an ancient term with a modern glow-up. Originally, it referred to local tyrants and landowners in imperial China—the petty strongmen who ruled villages through land ownership and brute force. After 1949, the Communist Party used it as a class enemy label. But in the 2010s, Chinese internet culture resurrected *tuhao* and gave it a new meaning: the newly wealthy who flaunt their money with spectacularly bad taste.

The word went viral in 2013 when a Chinese teenager posted a photo of her cat wearing an Apple Watch (on its paw, because the watch was too small for her wrist). The caption mocked "tuhao" behavior, and the meme exploded. Later that year, *tuhao* was shortlisted by the BBC as one of the year's most significant new words entering English.

## 🌍 Why It Matters

*Tuhao* matters because it fills a gap in English. "Nouveau riche" sounds French and literary. "New money" is bland. "Bling" describes the objects, not the person. *Tuhao* captures something specific: the combination of sudden wealth, rural or uncultured origins, and a complete lack of shame about showing off.

It also reflects a global anxiety. As China's economy produced millions of overnight millionaires, the West needed a word to describe them that was not simply "rich Chinese." *Tuhao* allowed English speakers to distinguish between old-money elegance and gold-plated excess.
