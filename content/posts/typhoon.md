Every year, the word *typhoon* blasts across news headlines from Manila to Miami. Most English speakers assume it is a generic meteorological term. In reality, it is a linguistic storm with multiple competing origins—and one of the strongest candidates speaks Chinese.

## 📖 Origin Story

The etymology of *typhoon* is a scholarly battlefield. Three theories compete:

1. **The Chinese theory:** From Cantonese *daai6 fung1* (大风, "big wind") or Mandarin *tai feng* (台风), recorded by Portuguese and Dutch navigators in the South China Sea during the 16th and 17th centuries.
2. **The Arabic theory:** From *tufan* (طوفان), meaning "flood" or "storm," which entered English via Indian Ocean trade routes.
3. **The Greek theory:** From *Typhon*, the monstrous father of hurricanes in Greek mythology.

The most likely answer is that all three converged. Sailors in the Age of Exploration were linguistic magpies, collecting words from every port. The Chinese *tai feng*, the Arabic *tufan*, and the Greek *Typhon* all described terrifying windstorms—and over centuries, they merged into the single word *typhoon*.

## 🌍 Why It Matters

*Typhoon* represents the early globalization of language. Long before the internet, sailors were already mixing Chinese, Arabic, and Greek into a shared vocabulary. Whether the word is "truly" Chinese depends on which linguist you ask, but the Chinese contribution is undeniable.

Today, the word also carries a geographic divide: *typhoon* refers to tropical cyclones in the Northwest Pacific, while *hurricane* refers to those in the Atlantic. The same phenomenon, split by language and longitude. It is a reminder that even nature must be named through culture.
