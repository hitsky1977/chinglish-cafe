💡 Did You Know?

📖

"Long time no see" first appeared in 1900.

Famously used in W.F. Drannan's book Thirty-One Years on the Plains and in the Mountains. Initially mocked by literary critics as grammatically incorrect, its catchy rhythm helped it spread rapidly across the globe, cementing its status as one of the earliest examples of Chinglish entering the mainstream English lexicon.

🏆

"Add oil" (加油) entered the Oxford Dictionary in 2018.

Recognized globally as a legitimate expression of encouragement by linguists, the official inclusion of this literal translation proves that Chinglish is no longer just considered "broken English". Instead, it is highly respected as a vibrant, evolving new dialect forming in real-time.

🧠

"Brainwash" is a direct translation from Chinese.

The English term "brainwash" is a literal calque of the Chinese word 洗脑 (xǐ nǎo). It was first used in English by journalist Edward Hunter in 1950 to describe how the Chinese army used psychological techniques during the Korean War, and has since become a universal psychological term.

🍅

"Ketchup" actually originated in Southern China.

Long before it became America's favorite tomato condiment, the word derived from the Hokkien Chinese word kê-tsiap (茄汁 / 鮭汁), which was originally a fermented fish sauce. British sailors brought the recipe and the name back to Europe in the 17th century.
